Degaussing & physical destruction explained

When a drive can't be erased — because it's failed, frozen, or locked — destruction is the answer. Here's what each method actually does.

t tScrub Team · Last updated 22 Sep 2026 · 5 min read

Under NIST 800-88, Destroy is the third outcome: rendering the media itself unusable so data can't be recovered. It's the last resort — used when Clear and Purge aren't possible, not as a first choice.

Degaussing

Degaussing subjects the media to a powerful magnetic field, scrambling the magnetic domains that store data. It works on magnetic media — hard drives and tapes. Crucially, it does not work on solid-state drives, which store data as electrical charge in flash cells rather than magnetic orientation.

A degaussed drive is usually unrecoverable — but also unusable, since the servo tracks that let the drive operate are typically destroyed along with the data.

Physical destruction

Physical destruction — shredding, disintegration, or crushing — works on everything, including SSDs. It's the universal answer for drives that can't be erased: failed drives, frozen drives, or drives locked behind Block SID lockdown.

The trade-off is irreversibility. A shredded drive can't be redeployed or resold, which is why destruction is a last resort rather than the default.

Erase vs destroy: the decision

SituationErase or destroy?
Healthy drive, firmware erase availableErase (Purge) — preserves resale value
Drive failed or physically damagedDestroy
Frozen / blocked, lock can't be liftedDestroy
Magnetic media, no erasure supportDegauss
SSD, no erasure supportDestroy (degaussing won't work)

FAQ

Can I degauss an SSD?

No. SSDs aren't magnetic, so a degausser has no effect on them. Physical destruction is the only destruction route for solid-state media.

Do I need a certificate for a destroyed drive?

If the drive can't be erased, you still want a record of its disposition — serial number, reason, and destruction date — so the audit trail doesn't have a hole in it.
